How to pass a prop firm challenge: the 10-point checklist

Most failed evaluations are not failed by bad strategies. They are failed by bad arithmetic, borrowed position sizes and a trader who never decided in advance what a normal loss looks like. Here is the checklist I give every funded member.

Key takeaways

  • Risk a fixed 1% or less per trade so five consecutive losers cannot breach a 5% daily limit.
  • Set the stop from market structure first, then calculate lot size — never the other way around.
  • Two A+ setups a day beats fifteen average ones, and it protects your consistency ratio.
  • Never chase back a losing day on the same session. The daily limit is a cliff, not a slope.
  • After a funded account is issued, your job changes: protect the split, not the target.

A funded-account evaluation is a risk-management exam dressed up as a trading contest. The target is achievable with almost any decent edge — 8% over an unlimited timeframe is not a heroic number. What kills accounts is the drawdown rulebook, and every one of the ten points below exists to keep you inside it.

1. Know your three numbers before you open the platform

You cannot trade a rule you have to look up. Write these on a sticky note before your first trade: your profit target in cash, your daily loss in cash and your maximum loss in cash. On a 100K 2 Step account that is $8,000 to clear phase one, $5,000 of daily breathing room and a $10,000 static floor. Percentages become real the moment they are in dollars.

2. Risk 1% per trade. Not 2%, not "1% of available margin"

With a 5% daily limit and 1% risk, you would need five consecutive full losses in a single day to breach. That almost never happens to a trader with a plan, but it happens constantly to a trader who doubles size after a loss. If your entries are less selective, drop to 0.5%. The maths is not the point; the breathing room is.

3. Calculate lot size from the stop, not the stop from the lot size

Beginners put on 1 lot and then look for a stop that "fits". Professionals mark the level that invalidates the idea, measure the distance in pips, and solve for size. We break the formula down with worked examples in position sizing: how to risk 1% correctly.

4. Pick two sessions and two setups only

Write down the exact session windows you will trade (London open, New York open) and the two setups you are allowed to take. Everything else is a paper trade. This single constraint removes most impulse entries, which removes most daily-limit breaches.

5. Respect the daily reset clock

Our daily loss limit resets at 00:00 UTC and is measured on closed equity. Know what that means in your timezone — 3am in Nairobi, 1am in Lagos — and know what your balance is at that moment, because that balance sets tomorrow's floor.

Worked example

Balance at reset: $102,400. Daily limit 5% = $5,120. The floor for that day is $97,280. If you risk 1% ($1,024) per trade with a 1:2 target, a single winner pays for two losers. You can have a genuinely bad day and still be fully alive tomorrow.

6. Stop trading the moment you are down 2%

A self-imposed soft stop at 2% of daily drawdown means you never actually test the hard limit. Two full losses and the laptop closes. This one rule has preserved more funded accounts than any indicator I have ever taught.

7. Keep the consistency ratio boring

On the 2 Step program, your best day cannot exceed 40% of total profit when a phase is verified. Traders get caught by this after a spectacular first day and then grind for two weeks to normalise the ratio. The fix is easy: cap your daily wins too. If you are up 3% by lunchtime, close the desk — you have already made a week's worth of money.

Most people who fail a challenge do not blow up dramatically. They bleed 1% a day being "active" until a 6% week turns into a 12% account.

8. Journal entries, not outcomes

Your journal should record the setup name, the level you traded from, the invalidation, the risk taken and a one-line note on whether you followed the plan — not the P&L. Traders who grade outcomes learn superstition. Traders who grade execution learn skill. Answer four questions daily: did I follow the plan, was the risk correct, was the setup in my playbook, and was my emotional state fit to trade?

9. Never average into a loser on an evaluation

Averaging down is a legitimate strategy with a defined maximum number of adds, in a funded account, over months — not on a two-phase evaluation with a hard floor. On a challenge, a losing position gets its stop respected or it gets closed manually. There is no third option.

10. Rehearse the funding transition before you pass

Funded accounts change the psychology, not the chart. Once you are trading for a split, a single good month pays real money, and that pressure produces over-risk. Decide now what your funded risk will be: usually the same 1% per trade, with a rule that any single trade risking more than 1.5% is a breach of your own process.

The five mistakes that end most evaluations

  1. Oversizing after two winners. Confidence is not a risk model.
  2. Revenge trading inside the same session after a stop-out, straight into the daily limit.
  3. Trading a news spike with a wide stop and a normal lot size, producing an abnormally large loss.
  4. Copying signals without knowing the invalidation level, so losses have no exit.
  5. Ignoring the reset time and holding a deep floating loss through the rollover.

None of these are strategy problems. They are all process problems, and process problems are fixed with a written plan and a checklist you actually use — which is exactly what the mentorship is built around if you want the structure handed to you.

Checklist FAQ

How many trades should I take per day on a challenge?

Two to four, maximum, with a daily loss soft stop at 2%. Traders who pass tend to take fewer and better trades than traders who fail, not more.

What win rate do I need to pass?

There is no required win rate. With a 1:2 risk-reward, a 40% win rate is profitable. What matters is that losers stay at 1% and winners are allowed to run to at least 2R.

Is it better to trade one instrument?

Yes for most people. Pick one or two instruments you know intimately — often XAU/USD and one FX major — and learn their session behaviour rather than scanning fifteen charts for action.

How long should a challenge take?

Most of our passing traders finish a 2 Step evaluation in two to five weeks. There is no time limit, so anything faster than that is usually a sign of oversizing rather than skill.
